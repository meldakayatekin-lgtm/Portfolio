About me

01 — background

I’m Melda, a biomedical engineering student at Stony Brook University. I’m drawn to medical device design because I want to help people do more of the things that matter to them. Working on assistive technology has taught me to ask questions I might have overlooked before. Can someone reach the button? Can they press it comfortably while they paint?

One of my favorite parts of engineering is taking a question like that into the makerspace and building something I can test. As I learn more about designing and testing medical devices, I want to work directly with the people who use them and get better at turning their feedback into changes that make a device easier to use.

VIP BEAR · Household team lead

Motorized countertop for limited reach

A United Cerebral Palsy (UCP) member needed a way to bring food from the back of a counter within reach. I led our team through a redesign after our first concept required too many parts. We printed a miniature turntable, bearing assembly, and motor housing, then tested rotation with and without weight.

Prototype tested

The next iteration scales the mechanism to an 18-inch turntable with a push-button control.

Motorized Toddler Mobility Trainer

Our team adapted a manual trainer for children with limited upper-body strength. A joystick controls a motor-driven roller that turns the existing wheel. Under load, traction at the roller–wheel interface limited performance, so we used contact force and motor torque to guide the mount design.

Microfluidic biosensor concept for Alzheimer’s biomarkers

For BME 100, I designed a concept for an implantable glass chip that would sample cerebrospinal fluid. The proposed channels route fluid to electrochemical sensors for Aβ40, Aβ42, and phosphorylated tau. The design received a faculty honorable mention.

VIP BEAR · Adaptive art

Head-tilt controller with micro:bit

I worked on a wearable controller for people who have difficulty using a handheld art tool. Our prototype uses a micro:bit mounted on a headband to translate head tilt into horizontal and vertical cursor movement. We tested the input on a computer as a first step toward guiding an airbrush on an X–Y gantry.

VIP BEAR · Adaptive art

Push-button Crayola airbrush

I helped adapt a Crayola airbrush for artists who have difficulty pressing its built-in button. We opened the housing, extended the switch wires, and connected a larger external push button so the artist could activate the airbrush from a position within reach.

Tikkun Olam Makers · Costume design and event coordination

Wheelchair-adaptive costume build event

I designed costume pieces for children who use wheelchairs, recruited makers to help build them, and coordinated the event. I fitted the pieces to the wheelchairs and revised the designs based on how they fit. The wheelchairs became part of each costume, from the body of the Ghostbusters car to the skyline beneath Spider-Man.
